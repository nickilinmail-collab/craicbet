Extra Places & Betting Offers: The Honest Maths

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 3 November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

"Extra places", "money back if your horse finishes second", "bet £10 get £30" — betting offers are the loudest part of the industry's shop window. Some carry real, measurable value. Most are worth less than the advert implies. This page is the honest-maths guide to telling them apart.

Extra-place races: the one genuine giveaway

In a standard each-way bet on a big handicap, the place part pays on the first four finishers. On designated "extra place" races — a staple on big Saturdays, at Cheltenham and at Galway — the firm pays five, six, sometimes more places at the same each-way fraction. That is a straightforward widening of your winning zone at no extra cost, and it is one of the few promotions where the value is arithmetic rather than marketing. The catch is that extra places usually come with minimum each-way terms (often 1/5 odds) and apply to specific races on specific days, not to your bet slip in general.

How to put a number on an extra place

Take a 20-runner handicap and a 16/1 shot you fancy to run well. At 1/5 the odds, the place part pays 16/5 — about 4.2 for a £1 place stake. One extra place raises your chance of collecting by roughly the chance your horse finishes exactly fifth. For a mid-market runner that is in the region of a few percent — small, but it is pure addition to the expected return, and across a season of big handicaps it compounds. The sharper the each-way terms already are for the race shape, the more the extra place is worth.

Money-back offers: read where the money goes

"Money back if your horse falls" or "refund if second to the SP favourite" sounds like insurance. The first question is always: money back as what? A cash refund is worth face value. A refund as a free bet is not — because a free bet does not return the stake on a winner, its fair value is roughly the free-bet amount multiplied by the chance of winning at the odds you take. As a rule of thumb, a £10 free bet used sensibly is worth £6–£7.50 in cash terms, and less if the terms force it onto short prices or split it into pieces.

Wagering strings: why "free" rarely is

Sign-up offers often arrive as a chain: deposit, stake at minimum odds, receive free bets, stake those at minimum odds again, sometimes with expiry windows of a few days. Every link in that chain costs you the bookmaker's margin. If each qualifying bet crosses a book with a 5% margin, a £30 package can leak a third of its headline value before you ever see withdrawable cash. None of this makes offers a con — it makes them a product with a price, and the price is in the terms, not the banner.

A simple checklist before taking any offer

Cash or free bet? Stake returned or stake not returned? Minimum odds, and are they higher than you would naturally bet? Expiry window? Maximum refund? Does it apply to the race you actually want, or only to a designated list? Five questions, thirty seconds, and the gloss comes off the bad offers very quickly. The good ones — genuine extra places on overbroad handicaps, cash refunds with no restrictions — survive the checklist comfortably.

Offers should subsidise betting you were going to do anyway, never bait you into staking more than planned. Set a budget and keep to it. Frequently Asked Questions

Are extra-place races genuinely good value?

Yes, usually — paying five or six places instead of four widens your winning zone at no extra cost, which is a real arithmetic gain rather than marketing. Check the each-way fraction (often 1/5) and that your race is actually on the designated list.

What is a free bet actually worth in cash?

Less than face value, because the stake is not returned on a winner. A £10 free bet used on reasonable odds is typically worth around £6–£7.50 in cash terms — and less if the terms force short prices or a quick expiry.

Why do offers have minimum odds and wagering conditions?

Because every qualifying bet you are required to place crosses the bookmaker's margin, which quietly claws back part of the offer's headline value. The conditions are the real price of the promotion — always read them before the banner.

How Does Each-Way Betting Work? Complete Irish Punter's Guide

Last updated: 5 November 2026

📝 Mark Craic, editor 📅 November 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

Each-way betting is one of the cornerstones of Irish punting culture, particularly in horse racing. Walk into any bookmakers before Cheltenham or the Grand National and you will hear the phrase constantly. Yet many punters who place each-way bets every week could not explain precisely how the payout is calculated. This guide lays it out clearly — the mechanics, the maths, the place terms, and the situations where each-way genuinely adds value versus when it quietly erodes it.

What Is an Each-Way Bet?

An each-way bet is two separate bets placed at the same time on the same selection: a win bet and a place bet. Because you are placing two bets, your stake is doubled. If you tell the cashier "€5 each-way", you are actually staking €10 — €5 on the win and €5 on the place.

The win part is straightforward: your horse must finish first. The place part pays out if your horse finishes within a specified number of positions — usually second or third, sometimes fourth, depending on the size of the field. The place odds are a fraction of the win odds, defined by the race's place terms.

Place Terms: How Many Places and What Fraction?

Place terms vary by race type and number of runners. The standard Horseracing Ireland (and UK Jockey Club) conventions used by Irish bookmakers are as follows:

  • 1–4 runners: No each-way market (win only).
  • 5–7 runners: 2 places paid, at 1/4 of the win odds.
  • 8–11 runners: 3 places paid, at 1/5 of the win odds.
  • 12–15 runners: 3 places paid, at 1/4 of the win odds.
  • 16+ runners (handicaps): 4 places paid, at 1/4 of the win odds.

These are standard terms. Individual bookmakers sometimes offer enhanced terms on big races — 5 or 6 places, or 1/4 odds on races that would normally pay 1/5. Always read the specific race terms on the slip or on-screen before you bet.

Calculating an Each-Way Payout: Worked Example

Say you back a horse at 12/1 each-way for €5 (total stake €10) in a 10-runner race. The standard terms are 3 places at 1/5 odds.

If the horse wins:

  • Win part: €5 × 12 = €60 profit + €5 stake back = €65
  • Place part: 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4/1. €5 × 2.4 = €12 profit + €5 stake back = €17
  • Total return: €82 (€72 profit from a €10 outlay)

If the horse finishes second or third (placed but not winning):

  • Win part: loses — €5 gone
  • Place part: €5 × 2.4 = €12 profit + €5 stake back = €17
  • Total return: €17 (€7 net profit from a €10 outlay)

If the horse finishes fourth or worse: Both parts lose — total loss €10.

When Does Each-Way Betting Offer Real Value?

Each-way value comes down to whether the place fraction is generous relative to the horse's true probability of placing. The key insight is this: on a short-priced horse in a small field, the place part is often overpriced in the bookmaker's favour, while on a longer-priced horse in a big field, it can genuinely represent value.

Consider a 6/4 favourite in an 8-runner race (3 places, 1/5 odds). The place portion pays just 6/4 ÷ 5 = 3/20 (roughly 15p profit per £1 placed). The true probability that a 6/4 shot finishes in the top 3 of an 8-runner race is very high — perhaps 80–85%. For 15p on the pound, you are paying a heavy price for near-certainty. By contrast, a 20/1 shot in a 20-runner handicap (4 places, 1/4 odds) pays 20/4 = 5/1 on the place, which can be excellent value if the horse genuinely has a live each-way chance.

Experienced each-way punters focus on big-field handicaps, where place terms are most generous relative to actual probabilities. Festival races with declared runners above 20 are the classic hunting ground for each-way value.

Each-Way on Non-Racing Markets

Some bookmakers extend each-way betting to golf (typically top 5 or top 8 finisher in a tournament) and occasionally to other sports. The same maths applies — a fraction of the win odds paid for finishing in a specified range. Golf each-way markets are popular for major championships, where field sizes of 150+ players make the place terms interesting on longer-priced players.

Each-way is rarely offered on football, tennis or other team/individual sports. If you do see it, check the terms carefully — the place fraction and number of places are set by the bookmaker and can vary considerably.

Common Mistakes with Each-Way Bets

The most common error is not knowing the place terms before placing the bet. If you back a horse each-way in a 7-runner race expecting 3 places and only 2 are paid, you lose the place part when your horse finishes third. Always confirm how many places are paid.

A second error is confusing the stake. "€5 each-way" costs €10. Many punters mentally budget for the stated amount rather than the full cost, which can cause account balance shocks.

Third: assuming each-way always reduces risk. On short-priced horses in small fields, the place part is a poor-value side bet that roughly halves your potential win profit without offering much real protection. Know when to bet win-only instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an each-way bet cost?

An each-way bet is two bets in one — a win bet and a place bet — so you pay double your stated stake. A €5 each-way bet costs €10 in total.

How many places does each-way cover in a horse race?

It depends on the number of runners. Races of 5–7 runners typically pay 2 places; 8–11 runners pay 3 places; 12–15 runners pay 3 places at 1/5 odds; handicaps of 16+ runners pay 4 places. Always check the terms before betting.

What does 1/4 odds a place mean?

Your horse's win odds are divided by 4 for the place part. If your horse is priced at 10/1 and finishes second, the place portion pays 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5/1 (plus your stake back).

Can I place each-way bets on football or other sports?

Each-way betting is primarily a horse racing and greyhound product. Some bookmakers offer each-way on golf (top 5 or top 8) and occasionally other sports, but it is rare outside racing.

Does each-way always offer value?

Not automatically. Each-way value depends on the place fraction and number of places relative to the horse's true chance. On short-priced favourites in small fields, the place part often costs more than its true worth.

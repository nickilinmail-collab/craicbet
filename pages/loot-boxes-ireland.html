Loot Boxes and Irish Law: A Parent's Guide

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 3 November 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

If your child plays FIFA-style football games, shooters or free-to-play mobile titles, they have almost certainly been offered a loot box: a paid-for package with randomised contents. This page explains what loot boxes are, where Irish law currently stands, and what a parent can practically do — with no betting pitch attached, because this is not a betting topic.

What a loot box actually is

A loot box is an in-game purchase whose contents are random: player packs, weapon skins, cosmetic items of varying rarity. The purchase mechanics mirror gambling closely — you pay a stake, you get an uncertain reward, and rare outcomes are deliberately engineered to be exciting. Many games layer this with virtual currencies bought in bundles, which blurs how much real money a purchase costs. A related ecosystem, skin gambling, lets items won in games be wagered on third-party sites — effectively unlicensed casinos using game items as chips.

Where Irish law stands

The Gambling Regulation Act 2024 regulates gambling for money or money's worth. The GRAI is reported to have concluded that video-game microtransactions such as loot boxes fall outside the Act as it stands, while keeping the matter under review — so loot boxes are currently not treated as licensed gambling products in Ireland, and no gambling-law age restriction applies to them. That can change: regulators across Europe are actively re-examining the question, and the GRAI's remit includes advising government on emerging risks.

How other countries compare

Belgium and the Netherlands have both treated certain paid loot boxes as gambling under their own laws, forcing publishers to remove or alter them in those markets. The UK has so far relied on industry self-regulation after concluding loot boxes fell outside its Gambling Act definitions. The international picture is a patchwork, which is exactly why Irish parents cannot assume the question is settled here.

Warning signs worth taking seriously

Most children buy the odd pack and move on. The patterns that deserve attention are the gambling-shaped ones: spending that escalates or is hidden, distress when a purchase "loses", chasing a rare item with repeated buys, money or gift cards going missing, and trading or wagering skins on outside websites. Research on young people repeatedly finds that heavy loot-box engagement correlates with problem-gambling indicators — correlation is not destiny, but it is a reason to pay attention early.

Practical parental controls

Every major platform lets you require a password or approval for every purchase, set monthly spending caps and view purchase history. Turn purchase approval on, keep cards out of saved payment methods, prefer gift-card top-ups with fixed amounts, and talk about odds plainly — a 1% drop rate means an expected hundred packs for one hit, which older kids can understand as maths rather than magic. For skin gambling specifically, the sites are unlicensed and off-limits to minors everywhere: treat them as a hard no.

If gambling-style harm has already appeared

Support exists and is free. The Gambling Awareness Trust funds confidential counselling for anyone affected by gambling harm — including family members, and including harm that started with games rather than bookmakers. Call 1800 753 753 or visit gamblingcare.ie. For under-18s, the HSE's youth mental-health services and your GP are also appropriate first doors. 18+ applies to all real-money gambling in Ireland.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are loot boxes classed as gambling in Ireland?

Not at present. The GRAI is reported to have concluded that loot boxes fall outside the Gambling Regulation Act 2024 as it stands, while keeping the matter under review. By contrast, Belgium and the Netherlands have treated certain paid loot boxes as gambling under their own laws.

What is skin gambling?

Wagering in-game items such as weapon skins on third-party websites — effectively unlicensed casinos using game items as chips. These sites hold no licence anywhere that protects an Irish user, and they are strictly off-limits for minors.

How do I stop my child buying loot boxes?

Turn on purchase approval or password protection on the console or phone, remove saved cards from the account, use fixed-value gift cards instead, set spending caps, and review purchase history together. Every major platform supports these controls in its family settings.

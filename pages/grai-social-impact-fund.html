The Social Impact Fund: Making Gambling Pay for Gambling Harm

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 4 min read

The Idea in One Sentence

Every GRAI-licensed operator pays a mandatory levy into a Social Impact Fund, which finances problem-gambling research, public awareness campaigns, and addiction treatment services — the industry funding the repair of the harm it can cause.

How the Levy Works

The Gambling Regulation Act 2024 establishes the fund and empowers the GRAI to set contributions, expected to be proportionate to each operator's scale. The precise levy rates are still to be prescribed — like licence fees were before S.I. 37/2026 set them. Once set, paying is a licence condition, with the usual sanctions behind it.

Where the Money Goes

Three destinations written into the Act: research into gambling harm in Ireland (long underfunded — Irish policy has leaned on UK data for years), awareness and education programmes, and treatment services for people harmed by gambling. Today that ecosystem runs largely on services like the Gambling Awareness Trust (1800 753 753, gamblingcare.ie).

Why This Matters to Players

Two reasons. First, it is another cost of being licensed — operators who pay it are invested in the Irish system, visible on the register. Second, it means independent, Irish-specific research into what actually causes harm — which over time shapes smarter rules than imported assumptions. Offshore operators contribute nothing to any of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Social Impact Fund?

A fund established by the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, financed by a mandatory levy on licensed operators, paying for problem-gambling research, awareness and treatment in Ireland.

How much do operators pay into the fund?

The levy rates are still to be prescribed by regulation; contributions are expected to scale with operator size — as happened with licence fees.

Do offshore casinos pay into it?

No — only GRAI-licensed operators. Offshore operators serving Irish players contribute nothing to Irish harm-reduction funding.

Irish Grand National Betting Guide: Easter Monday at Fairyhouse

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

The Irish Grand National is the biggest betting race run on Irish soil: a marathon handicap chase at Fairyhouse every Easter Monday, first run in 1870, and a race where the romance and the betting maths pull in opposite directions. This guide covers how to bet it without kidding yourself.

The race in one paragraph

Around three miles five furlongs over Fairyhouse’s fences, a field that can run up to 30 strong, and handicap conditions — every horse weighted with the aim of making the finish a photo. Arkle famously won it in 1964 under 12 stone; most years it goes to a progressive staying chaser the handicapper hasn’t caught up with yet. It is Ireland’s Easter institution, and the betting turnover reflects it.

Big-field handicaps are the bookmaker’s favourite race too

The honest maths first: the more runners, the more margin. Price up 25–30 horses and the overround on a race like this is usually far fatter than on an eight-runner Grade 1 — often several percentage points per runner. That doesn’t make the race unbettable; it means price-shopping and timing matter more here than almost anywhere else in the Irish calendar.

Each-way terms are where the value moves

Standard each-way terms on a big handicap are a quarter the odds, first four places. For the Irish Grand National most firms go further on the day with extra places — five, six, sometimes more. Extra places are a genuine concession, but check the fraction: a fifth of the odds with six places can pay less on a 3rd-place finish than a quarter with four. Ante-post or on the day?

Weeks out you get bigger prices and the risk of losing your stake if the horse doesn’t run — standard ante-post rules are all-in, run or not. Closer to the race, non-runner offers and guaranteed prices shrink that risk for shorter odds. The trade-off is covered in our ante-post guide; the short version for a weights-sensitive handicap is that early prices only make sense once the weights are published and you can judge who is thrown in.

Reading the trends without worshipping them

Trends articles will tell you the winner is usually a second-season chaser, lightly weighted, aged seven to nine. Treat those as descriptions of the past, not laws: with 30 runners, “profile fits” narrows a list, it doesn’t find a winner. Nobody’s shortlist makes this race safe, and no tip — ours included — is a certainty. Frequently Asked Questions

When is the Irish Grand National run?

On Easter Monday at Fairyhouse, Co. Meath, as the highlight of the Easter Festival. Because Easter moves, the date changes each year; Easter Monday 2027 falls on 29 March.

What are the usual each-way terms for the Irish Grand National?

The standard is a quarter of the odds, first four places. Many bookmakers offer extra places on the day; always check the place fraction as well as the number of places, since a fifth of the odds can pay less than a quarter.

Is ante-post betting on the race worth it?

Only after the weights are published and only with money you accept losing to a non-runner, since standard ante-post bets are not refunded if the horse doesn’t line up. Non-runner offers closer to the race remove that risk at shorter prices.

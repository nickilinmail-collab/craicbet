How Golf Betting Works

Golf betting covers a wider range of markets than many punters realise. The main options available on most GRAI-licensed sites include:

  • Tournament outright winner — Bet on who wins the tournament before it begins. These are usually offered with each-way terms, particularly for Majors. With fields of 100–160 players, ante-post outright prices can be long, especially for mid-ranked players.
  • Each-way — The most popular golf market in Ireland. Covers both a win and a place finish (see section below for full details).
  • Matchplay and head-to-head — Two players matched against each other regardless of the rest of the field. The player with the better score (or who advances further in matchplay knockout events) wins the bet. Bookmakers set a line — a handicap — if one player is significantly stronger.
  • Make or miss the cut — After 36 holes, the lowest-scoring half of the field (plus ties) progress. You bet on whether a specific player makes or misses this cut line.
  • First round leader — Which player posts the lowest score in round one? A popular speculative market with enhanced odds.
  • Hole-in-one markets — Will a hole-in-one be recorded during the tournament? Available as a yes/no market at some bookmakers.
  • Top nationality — Which player from a named nationality (e.g. top Irish, top European) finishes highest? Particularly popular during Ryder Cup cycles and Major weeks.
House margin note: Golf outright markets typically carry a higher bookmaker margin than a football match result, because the field is large and odds are hard to set precisely. Where possible, compare each-way terms across GRAI-licensed sites before placing — the difference in place fractions and number of places can materially affect your return.

Each-Way Betting in Golf

Each-way is by far the most popular way to bet on golf in Ireland, particularly for the four Majors. Its appeal comes from the nature of the sport: large fields mean long outright prices, and an each-way bet means you can still return a profit if your player finishes in the top 5, 6, 7 or 8 — even if they never contend for the title.

How the bet works: An each-way bet is two equal stakes — one on the win, one on the place. If your player wins, both parts pay. If they finish inside the place positions (but not first), only the place part pays out at a fraction of the win odds. If they finish outside the places, both parts of the bet lose.

Place terms vary between bookmakers and between tournaments. Common terms for golf:

  • Major championships: typically 1/4 odds, top 8 places
  • Rolex Series / Elevated events: often 1/4 odds, top 5 or 6
  • Standard tour events: often 1/4 odds, top 5
  • Smaller events (sub-78 player field): sometimes 1/5 odds, top 4

Some bookmakers run weekly promotions offering extra places — for example, paying 10 places instead of 8 for a Major. These can significantly improve the value of an each-way bet but always come with specific conditions. Check terms before placing. For a full worked example with the settlement maths, see our dedicated golf each-way guide.

Dead heat rule in golf: If two or more players tie for the last place position (e.g. three players tie for 8th with only 8 places paying), the dead heat rule applies. Your each-way place return is divided by the number of players sharing that position relative to the number of place positions they are sharing. The dead heat guide explains the calculation in full.

Major Championship Calendar

The four Major championships are the peak of golf's annual calendar and attract the highest betting volumes. Each has its own character, course type and history. Ante-post markets for Majors typically open months in advance, with the longest prices available well before the field is confirmed.

Each-way terms for Majors (usually 1/4, 8 places) are generally the most generous of any golf event — some bookmakers extend to 10 places for Major weeks as a promotion. Always check each-way terms before placing, as they differ by operator.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is each-way betting in golf?

Each-way betting in golf is a two-part bet: one part on the golfer to win the tournament outright, and one part on them to finish inside a specified number of places — typically the top 5, 6, 7 or 8 depending on the event. If your player wins, both parts pay out. If they finish in a place but not first, only the place part pays at a fraction of the win odds (usually 1/4 or 1/5). If they finish outside the places, both parts lose. See the full golf each-way guide for settlement examples.

How many places does each-way pay in golf tournaments?

The number of each-way places varies by tournament type and by bookmaker. For the four Major championships, most bookmakers offer 1/4 odds for the top 8 places as standard, with some offering 10 places as a weekly promotion. For regular tour events, 5 places at 1/4 odds is common. Smaller events with fewer than 78 players may offer only 4 places. Always check the each-way terms listed on the market before placing your bet, as they can differ significantly between operators.

What are the best markets for golf betting in Ireland?

The most popular golf betting markets for Irish punters are the tournament outright each-way, top nationality (e.g. top Irish or top European), head-to-head matchplay markets, make or miss the cut, and first round leader. Each-way outright bets on longer-priced players remain especially popular because of the large fields and consequent big ante-post prices. See the full breakdown in our golf betting guide.

When is the best time to bet on golf tournaments?

Many punters prefer to place outright and each-way bets early in the week before Thursday’s first round, when odds on players tend to be at their longest. Prices typically shorten as the field is confirmed and weather forecasts emerge. Ante-post markets for Major championships open months in advance with the longest prices. In-play markets open once the round begins and move rapidly based on live scoring — these require quick decisions and close attention to the leaderboard.

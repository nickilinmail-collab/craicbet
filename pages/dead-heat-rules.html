Dead Heat Rules: the Maths of a Tie, Explained

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

A dead heat is the result the camera cannot split: two horses on the line, five golfers tied for fourth. Nothing in betting generates more “I’ve been short-changed” emails, and almost none of them are right — the settlement is a standard rule applied with ordinary arithmetic. Here is that arithmetic, with worked examples.

The rule in one sentence

In a dead heat, your stake is divided by the number of competitors who tied, and the surviving fraction is paid at the full odds you took. The odds are never cut; the stake is. Two-way tie: half your stake wins, half is treated as lost. Three-way: a third wins.

Worked example: the two-way photo

You have €10 on a horse at 4.00 (3/1) and it dead-heats for first with one other. Settlement: €5 (half the stake) at 4.00 = €20 return, for a €10 profit instead of the €30 you’d have made winning outright. The other €5 is settled as a loser. Punters sometimes read the €20 as “half odds” — the economics are identical, but the rule as written divides stake, which is what you’ll see on the settled slip.

Golf: where dead heats live

Dead heats are an edge case in racing but routine in golf, because place terms pay several positions and strokeplay ties constantly. The refinement: your stake is multiplied by places remaining ÷ players tied. Say your each-way bet pays five places, three players finish clearly ahead, and four players tie for fourth — four players for the two remaining paid places. The place part of your stake is settled at 2/4 (half) at full place odds. Every Sunday evening, somewhere, a golf punter is doing exactly this sum.

The each-way interaction

An each-way bet is two bets, and a dead heat can touch either or both. Take €10 each-way at 41.0 (40/1), a fifth of the odds for five places (place part: 9.0). Your golfer ties fourth with three others, as above: the win part loses in full, and the place part is €10 × 2/4 = €5 at 9.0 = €45 return from €20 total staked. Disappointing after watching him lead on Saturday; correct to the cent. Our each-way calculator handles the fractions if you’d rather not.

Where the rule does not apply

Dead-heat division is for ties the event itself does not break. If the competition has its own tiebreaker — a playoff for the golf title, countback in some sports — the market follows the official result and no division happens on the outright winner. That is why you can lose a “to win the tournament” bet in a playoff while your tied-position place bet still pays dead-heat fractions on where the playoff loser officially finished. Bookmakers’ house rules spell out which markets follow which results; the two minutes reading them beats the email afterwards.

Honest conclusions

Dead-heat settlement is one of the fairer corners of betting: a transparent formula, applied identically to everyone, that pays exactly the expected value of a tie. Frequently Asked Questions

How is a dead heat paid out?

Your stake is divided by the number of tied competitors and the surviving fraction is paid at full odds. €10 at 4.00 in a two-way dead heat settles as €5 at 4.00 — a €20 return.

How do dead heats work in golf each-way betting?

The place stake is multiplied by places remaining ÷ players tied. Four players tied for fourth with two paid places left means half the place stake settles at full place odds and the rest is lost.

Does a playoff count as a dead heat?

No. When the event breaks the tie itself — a golf playoff for the title — winner markets follow the official result, though tied finishing positions can still settle as dead heats in place markets.

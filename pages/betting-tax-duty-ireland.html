Betting Duty in Ireland: Who Actually Pays the 2%

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

Two tax facts shape betting in Ireland, and most punters only half-know them. First: your winnings are not taxed — not on a winning docket, not on a six-figure acca, not ever. Second: the State still takes a cut of every bet, because the bookmaker pays a 2% duty on your stake. Here is how the system works, where it came from, and who really carries the cost.

Your winnings are tax-free

Gambling winnings in Ireland are not subject to income tax, capital gains tax or USC, and you do not declare them on a return. The logic is old and practical: betting is not treated as a trade, and Revenue taxes the activity at the point of the stake instead. One footnote worth knowing — the winnings themselves are free, but anything they earn afterwards is not. Put a big win on deposit and the interest is taxable like any other interest.

The 2% duty — paid behind the counter, not at it

Since 2019, licensed bookmakers pay betting duty of 2% of the stake on every bet taken from customers in Ireland, in a shop or online. The punter is never invoiced: you stake €10, the bookmaker owes Revenue 20 cent. Betting exchanges are taxed differently — a betting intermediary duty of 25% applies to the commission they charge Irish customers, because the exchange never holds the bet itself. The 2019 change doubled the main rate from 1%, and the Irish Bookmakers Association has linked that rise to a wave of shop closures in the years that followed.

From 20% on the docket to 2% in the books

Older punters remember paying betting tax themselves. In the early 1980s the on-course and shop duty ran as high as 20%, charged openly on the slip — and it pushed serious turnover towards illegal operators. Governments spent the next two decades cutting it in stages; by 2006 the rate stood at just 1%, and along the way the legal burden migrated from the punter’s docket to the bookmaker’s accounts. The lesson Irish policy took from the 20% era still echoes in today’s debates: tax betting too hard and the betting does not stop, it just stops being visible.

Budget 2027: the rise that didn’t happen

Through 2026 a duty increase was openly on the table — government papers floated raising the 2% rate to help fund Budget 2027, and bookmakers lobbied hard against it, warning of black-market leakage and more shop closures. In the event, the Budget delivered in October 2026 left betting duty unchanged at 2% for 2027. What is coming instead is the Social Impact Fund: a separate levy on licensed operators under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, with the rate still to be set — we track it in our Social Impact Fund guide.

Where the money goes

Betting duty receipts have long been associated with funding racing. The Horse and Greyhound Racing Fund rises to €102 million in 2027 — about 80% to horse racing, the rest to greyhounds — so a slice of every stake effectively loops back into the industry punters bet on.

The honest maths: you pay it anyway

“The bookmaker pays the duty” is legally true and economically half-true. A 2% tax on turnover is a cost of goods, and costs end up in prices — which for a bookmaker means the overround. Ireland taxes stakes; the UK instead taxes bookmaker gross profits at 15%. Different bases, same destination: the margin you bet into reflects the operator’s tax bill. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to declare betting winnings to Revenue in Ireland?

No. Gambling winnings are not subject to income tax, capital gains tax or USC in Ireland and do not go on a tax return. Interest or investment income earned on winnings afterwards is taxable as normal.

Who pays Ireland’s 2% betting duty?

The licensed bookmaker pays it on the stake of every bet taken from customers in Ireland, in shops and online. Betting exchanges pay a separate 25% duty on the commission they charge. Punters are not charged directly, though the cost feeds into bookmaker margins.

Did Budget 2027 increase betting duty?

No. A rise was considered during 2026, but the Budget delivered in October 2026 kept betting duty at 2% of stakes for 2027. A separate Social Impact Fund levy on licensed operators is expected under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, with the rate still to be set.

GRAI Targets 100+ Unlicensed Betting Sites: What Irish Bettors Need to Know

Published: 5 November 2026
📝 Mark Craic, editor 📅 November 2026 ⏱ 4 min read

The Gambling Regulatory Authority of Ireland (GRAI) has ramped up enforcement action against unlicensed betting operators, with more than 100 sites in the crosshairs of its compliance programme as of November 2026. For Irish punters, that number is both reassuring and a pointed reminder: if a site you use is not on the GRAI public register, it is operating outside the law and beyond the reach of consumer protections the new framework was designed to deliver. 18+ only. No bet is guaranteed.

What the Enforcement Programme Looks Like

GRAI began issuing remote betting licences on 1 July 2026 under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024. From that date, any operator accepting bets from Irish customers online was required to hold a valid GRAI remote betting licence or cease taking Irish customers. Around 41 remote betting licences had been issued by November 2026 — a significant but limited share of the total market. Many operators that have served Irish punters for years have not yet completed the licensing process, and some have shown no intention of doing so.

GRAI's enforcement programme targets operators in that unlicensed space. The authority has powers to issue compliance notices, pursue court orders blocking access to sites from Ireland, and — where operators are based in jurisdictions with mutual recognition arrangements — seek co-operation from overseas regulators. The most visible outcome so far was the blocking of Stake.com for Irish users in September 2026 following GRAI intervention. Stake, which had a substantial Irish user base, did not hold a GRAI licence and was among the first high-profile operators to be shut out of the Irish market through enforcement rather than voluntary withdrawal.

Why This Matters for Irish Punters

Using an unlicensed betting site is not a criminal offence for the individual punter — the legal obligation falls on the operator. But the practical risks for bettors are real. An unlicensed site:

  • Is not required to offer deposit limits, spending alerts or cooling-off periods
  • Has no obligation to participate in the National Gambling Exclusion Register (NGER)
  • Cannot be reported to GRAI if a dispute arises — the authority has no jurisdiction over it
  • Is not required to segregate customer funds, so money held in your account carries additional risk if the operator ceases trading
  • May continue showing gambling advertising to Irish users outside the permitted hours set by GRAI's watershed rules (no gambling ads before 9pm)

None of that means every unlicensed site is a scam — many are established international operators that have simply not completed Irish licensing. But the absence of a GRAI licence means the structural protections the Act created are simply not in place for your account.

How to Check if a Site Is Licensed

The quickest way is the public licence register at grai.ie. The register lists every GRAI licence by number, operator name, licence type and validity period. The licence type matters: GRAI currently issues remote betting licences only. It has not issued any casino or gaming licences — those are a separate category not expected until 2027–2028. Any site claiming a GRAI casino licence as of now should be treated with serious scepticism.

When checking, look for the licence number format (e.g. GRAI-XXXX-RB-26-XXXX, where "RB" denotes remote betting) and confirm the expiry date is in the future. The National Gambling Exclusion Register (NGER)

One of the most important tools GRAI has introduced is the National Gambling Exclusion Register, which is now live. The NGER allows any Irish bettor to self-exclude from all GRAI-licensed operators with a single application submitted via grai.ie — rather than having to contact each operator individually. All licensed bookmakers are required to check the register and enforce exclusions. This is a meaningful upgrade from the patchwork of operator-level self-exclusion that existed before.

If you use an unlicensed site, the NGER does not apply — the operator has no obligation to check it. That is another concrete reason why the GRAI enforcement programme, however gradual, matters for punters who rely on self-exclusion as a safeguard.

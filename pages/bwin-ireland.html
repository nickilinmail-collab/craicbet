What Is bwin?

bwin is one of Europe’s most established online bookmakers, with roots going back to the late 1990s. It is now part of Entain plc, the FTSE 100 gambling group that also owns Coral and Ladbrokes in Ireland and the UK, as well as a range of other brands across Europe and North America.

bwin has historically been particularly strong in Germany, Austria, and Belgium, and maintains a significant presence across Spain, Italy, and other regulated European markets. It is known for competitive football markets, live betting features, and a broad range of sports. The platform holds licences from the Malta Gaming Authority (MGA) and the UK Gambling Commission (UKGC) — both reputable regulators — and is a legitimately operating bookmaker in its licensed markets.

That said, holding an MGA or UKGC licence does not automatically authorise an operator to target Irish customers under Ireland’s new regulatory framework. Since 1 July 2026, that requires a separate GRAI remote betting licence.

bwin’s GRAI Licence Status in Ireland

Ireland’s Gambling Regulation Act 2024 established the Gambling Regulatory Authority of Ireland (GRAI) as the country’s dedicated gambling regulator. The GRAI began issuing remote betting licences from 1 July 2026. Any operator wishing to legally target Irish customers with betting products must hold one of these licences.

As of this writing in November 2026, bwin does not appear on the GRAI’s public register of licensed remote betting operators. This means bwin has not been granted a GRAI remote betting licence and is not currently operating under Irish regulatory authorisation.

Can Irish Players Use bwin?

bwin may remain technically accessible to users with Irish IP addresses via its MGA licence, depending on whether bwin has implemented any geographic restrictions for the Irish market. However, accessible does not mean licensed, and there is a meaningful practical difference for players.

When you use a bookmaker that holds a GRAI licence, you are covered by Irish-specific regulatory requirements. When you use an operator without a GRAI licence — regardless of what other licences it holds — those Irish protections do not apply to your account. Specifically, without a GRAI licence:

  • Deposit limit enforcement: GRAI-licensed operators must enforce deposit limits as required under Irish regulations. An operator outside the GRAI framework is not bound by these requirements.
  • NGEI integration: GRAI licensees must integrate with the National Gambling Exclusion Identification (NGEI) register, Ireland’s national self-exclusion scheme. A non-GRAI operator is not required to check or honour NGEI exclusions.
  • Irish complaints escalation: If you have a dispute with a GRAI-licensed operator that cannot be resolved directly, you can escalate it to the GRAI. No such formal Irish route exists for a non-GRAI operator.
  • Credit card ban compliance: Irish law bans credit card use for gambling with GRAI-licensed operators. This requirement does not bind a non-GRAI operator, so you might technically be able to use a credit card — a form of gambling with borrowed money that Irish regulators have specifically prohibited.
  • GRAI-mandated withdrawal timelines: GRAI licensees must meet Irish-regulated payout standards. Non-GRAI operators are governed only by their own terms and their home regulator.

None of this is intended to suggest bwin is an unsafe operator in general terms — its MGA and UKGC licences provide substantial protections. But Irish players considering bwin should be clear that they would be operating outside Irish regulatory oversight.

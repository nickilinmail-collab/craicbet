The Irish Racing Calendar: Key Dates

Irish horse racing runs year-round. These are the moments that define the betting calendar:

  • January–February: Early jumps season at Leopardstown and Navan. Ante-post markets for Cheltenham open — best long-game value.
  • March (Cheltenham Week): The defining week of the Irish racing year. Irish-trained horses dominate; the Gold Cup, Champion Hurdle, and Champion Chase draw the biggest Irish betting volumes of any event.
  • Easter Weekend (April): Irish Grand National at Fairyhouse. 30-runner field; each-way betting essential.
  • Late April–May (Punchestown Festival): National Hunt's Irish closing statement. Rematches from Cheltenham, Grade 1s across five days.
  • May–September (Flat season): The Curragh Classics, Irish Champions Weekend in September.
  • July (Galway Festival): Seven days of Flat and Jumps at Galway; popular and social, with both serious handicaps and commercial races.
  • December (Leopardstown Christmas Festival): Grade 1 jumps over four days; King George prep races and Drinmore Novice Chase.

How to Read Irish Racing Form

Irish racing form figures follow a standard format used across UK and Irish racing: numbers from left to right show most recent (rightmost) to oldest (leftmost) race finishes within a season, separated by a dash for each new season. Key codes:

  • 1–9: Finishing position
  • 0: 10th or worse
  • F: Fell (jumps racing)
  • U: Unseated rider
  • P: Pulled up (did not finish)
  • R: Refused (at a fence or hurdle)
  • BD: Brought down (by another fallen horse)
  • -: Season separator
  • /: Separates by multiple seasons ago

For example: 2/1-114F means: 2nd two seasons ago (separated by /), then 1st the previous season, then in the current season: 1st, 1st, 4th, and Fell. A string of Ps suggests a horse that struggles to complete, which matters enormously in jumps betting — you cannot back a horse each-way if it rarely finishes the race.

Irish Horse Racing Bet Types

Win Single

The simplest bet: pick a horse to win its race. The implied probability in the odds tells you what the bookmaker estimates the horse's winning chance to be. At 5/1 (6.00 decimal), the implied probability is 1/6 = 16.7%. If you think the true probability is higher than 16.7%, you have a value bet. The bookmaker's margin (typically 15–20% in a full racing field) means the sum of implied probabilities exceeds 100% — they build in an edge across the whole field, not just your selection.

Each-Way

Each-way splits your stake equally between win and place. The place terms vary by field size:

  • 5–7 runners: win only (no place market) or 1/4 odds, 2 places
  • 8–11 runners: 1/5 odds, 3 places
  • 12–15 runners: 1/4 odds, 3 places
  • 16+ runners (handicap): 1/4 odds, 4 places
  • Large handicaps (20+ runners): some bookmakers offer 5 places

Each-way is most valuable when the place part pays at good terms relative to the horse's true finishing probability. A 20/1 shot finishing top 4 at 1/4 odds returns 5/1 on the place part — solid value if you thought the horse had a 25%+ chance of placing.

Accumulator (Acca)

An accumulator combines multiple selections into a single bet where all must win. The odds multiply. A four-horse acca at 2/1, 3/1, 5/2, and 7/2 returns roughly 90/1 — attractive, but all four selections must win. Racing accas have a poor expected value for most punters because bookmakers apply Rule 4 deductions and non-runner rules that can significantly reduce returns. Ante-Post Betting

Ante-post bets are placed well before the event — sometimes months in advance. The advantage: much better odds than close-to-race prices. The risk: if your horse is a non-runner (withdrawn), you lose your stake unless Non-Runner No Bet (NRNB) terms apply. Always check whether NRNB is offered before placing ante-post bets, especially on novice horses whose plans can change significantly.

Form Factors That Matter Most in Irish Racing

  • Going (ground conditions): Irish racing is significantly affected by rainfall. A horse that excels on Good to Firm ground may be entirely different on Heavy. Check the declared going the morning of racing and cross-reference with the horse's form figures on similar going.
  • Trainer form: Irish racing has a small number of elite training operations that dominate Grade 1 racing. When a leading handler sends a horse to a specific meeting, particularly a lesser fixture with a small team, it often signals a planned tilt at the race. Watch for trainer strike rates at specific tracks.
  • Jockey bookings: A change to a retained top jockey on a horse previously ridden by a conditional (apprentice/claim) rider is a significant market signal. Jockey bookings are published the day before racing.
  • Market moves: Significant morning-line market moves (a horse drifting from 7/2 to 6/1, or shortening from 8/1 to 4/1) often reflect stable confidence (shortening) or stable concerns/non-ideal preparation (drifting). Track opening and closing prices.
  • Weight in handicaps: In handicap races, every pound of weight is estimated to be worth approximately one length over a mile. A well-handicapped horse (top weight, but whose official rating undersells its true ability) is the classic value target in Irish handicaps.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main Irish racecourses?

The most important for punters are: Leopardstown (Dublin, Grade 1 flat and jumps, Christmas Festival); The Curragh (Kildare, home of the Irish Classics); Galway (summer festival, July); Fairyhouse (Irish Grand National, Easter); Punchestown (National Hunt Festival, April–May); Navan, Cork, and Tipperary for regular fixtures. Ireland has 26 licensed racecourses in total.

What is each-way betting in horse racing?

Each-way splits your stake in two: half on the horse to win, half on it to place (finish in a set number of positions, usually top 3 or 4 depending on field size). The place part pays a fraction of win odds — typically 1/4 or 1/5. Each-way is most valuable in large fields with good place terms.

What is Best Odds Guaranteed (BOG)?

Best Odds Guaranteed means that if the starting price (SP) at race time is higher than the price you took in the morning, the bookmaker pays you at the SP. Significant benefit in Irish racing where morning prices can drift dramatically. Not all GRAI-licensed bookmakers offer BOG — check before you bet.

What does Non-Runner No Bet mean?

Non-Runner No Bet means your stake is returned in full as cash if your selection is withdrawn. Unlike standard Rule 4 deductions (which reduce your winnings), NRNB gives you a full refund. Essential for ante-post betting where withdrawals are common. Always check the specific NRNB terms before placing ante-post bets.

How do I read Irish racing form?

Form figures show finishing positions from left (oldest) to right (most recent) within a season. Key codes: F = fell, U = unseated, P = pulled up, R = refused, BD = brought down. A dash (-) separates seasons. Example: 2-114 means 2nd last season, then 1st, 1st, 4th this season. For jumps horses, avoid horses with frequent P codes (rarely finishes) for each-way bets.

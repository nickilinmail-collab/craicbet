Betting Tax in Ireland: What You Need to Know

Last updated: 4 November 2026

📝 Mark Craic, editor 📅 Updated November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read
Short answer: Irish punters pay no tax on betting winnings. The 1% betting duty is paid by licensed operators, not players. For specific tax advice about your situation, consult a qualified accountant.

One of the most common questions from Irish punters is whether they need to declare or pay tax on betting winnings. The answer, for the vast majority of recreational bettors, is no. This guide explains the Irish betting tax situation, how it compares to the UK, what operators pay, and the narrow circumstances where professional gamblers may face different considerations.

Irish Punters Pay No Tax on Winnings

Betting winnings are not subject to income tax in Ireland for recreational punters. You do not need to declare regular betting winnings to Revenue. There is no capital gains tax on gambling profits. Ireland has never taxed punter winnings in the way some jurisdictions do — this is not a recent change, it is simply how Irish tax law treats gambling income for players.

This is a meaningful benefit compared to some other countries. In parts of the United States, for example, gambling winnings above certain thresholds must be declared. In Ireland, no such requirement exists for recreational bettors.

The common misconception that you must declare large winnings appears to come from general awareness of tax on other forms of income. It is understandable — a large win can feel like it should be taxable. But under current Irish tax law, it is not.

The 1% Betting Duty: Paid by Operators, Not You

Ireland does have a betting tax — it is just not paid by punters. Licensed bookmakers pay a 1% betting duty on stakes placed through their platforms, collected by Revenue. This duty applies to bets placed with GRAI-licensed operators.

This operator-side levy is built into bookmakers' cost structures and effectively recovered through the margins they set on odds. When a bookmaker builds in a 5% overround on a football market, the 1% betting duty is one of the costs that margin covers. You are indirectly contributing to it through the margin — but you are not writing a cheque to Revenue.

This structure — duty on the operator, not the punter — has been the model in Ireland for some time. The UK moved to a similar model in 2001 when it abolished the 9% punter tax and shifted the burden entirely to operators (who now pay 15% Remote Gaming Duty on their gross profits). Both jurisdictions reached the same conclusion: taxing punter winnings discourages betting with licensed domestic operators, which reduces the tax base. Taxing operators' revenue is more efficient and encourages customers to use regulated platforms.

GRAI Licensing Fees: Also Borne by Operators

The Gambling Regulatory Authority of Ireland (GRAI) charges licensing fees to operators. A remote betting licence (like GRAI-0191-RB-26-0001, held by our recommended partner) carries application and renewal costs that are borne entirely by the operator. These fees fund the GRAI's regulatory activities, including the register maintenance, compliance monitoring, and complaints process.

As with betting duty, these costs are operational expenses for the bookmaker, not a player-facing charge. They contribute to the overall cost base that bookmakers factor into their margin setting — but again, you do not pay them directly.

Professional Gamblers: A Different Consideration

Revenue's general position is that betting winnings are not trading income. However, Irish tax law does not contain an absolute legislative exemption for all gambling activity in all circumstances. In practice, courts and Revenue in Ireland (and the UK) have consistently found that gambling is not a trade and winnings are not taxable. But the question of whether highly systematic, professional-level gambling could ever be characterised as a trade remains a nuanced legal area.

For the overwhelming majority of Irish punters — recreational bettors spending leisure funds on sports betting — this is not a live concern. If you are placing bets at significant scale, treating it as a business activity, or generating income you depend on, it would be prudent to take specific advice from a qualified accountant or tax adviser. VAT on Betting

Betting services in Ireland are exempt from VAT. This is consistent with the EU VAT Directive, which allows member states to exempt betting and lotteries from VAT. Online bookmakers do not charge VAT on stakes or winnings, and you will not see a VAT component in your betting account activity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I pay tax on betting winnings in Ireland?

No. Betting winnings are completely tax-free for recreational punters in Ireland. There is no requirement to declare betting winnings to Revenue for income tax purposes if you are a recreational bettor. The 1% betting duty on stakes is paid by licensed operators, not by players.

Who pays the betting duty in Ireland?

Licensed bookmakers pay the 1% betting duty to Revenue on bets placed through their platforms. This is an operator-side levy, not a player tax. It is built into the bookmaker's operating costs and effectively factored into the margins they set on their odds.

Is the Irish tax situation different from the UK?

Both Ireland and the UK treat betting winnings as tax-free for players. The UK abolished a 9% punter tax in 2001 when the duty shifted entirely to operators. In Ireland, the duty on betting stakes is 1%, paid by operators. In both countries, winnings are not income for tax purposes for recreational bettors.

Could a professional gambler owe tax in Ireland?

This is a nuanced area. Revenue's general position is that betting winnings are not trading income. However, there is no absolute legislative exemption for all gambling activity. If you bet at significant scale or treat it as a business, consult a qualified accountant or tax adviser. This guide does not constitute tax advice.

Do GRAI licensing fees affect the odds I receive?

GRAI licensing fees are borne by operators, not players. However, all operator-side costs — licensing fees, betting duty, operational costs — are factored into the margins bookmakers build into their odds. This is why comparing margins across bookmakers is worthwhile.

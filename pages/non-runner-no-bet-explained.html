Non-Runner No Bet Explained: NRNB vs Ante-Post All-In

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 3 November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

Back a horse in January for the Cheltenham Festival and one question matters more than the price: what happens to your money if the horse never lines up? The answer depends on whether the bet was struck ante-post all-in or Non-Runner No Bet — NRNB — and the difference is the whole game.

Ante-post all-in: the default, and the risk

A standard ante-post bet is "all-in, run or not". If your selection is injured, re-routed to a different race or simply not declared, the stake is lost. That is not small print — it is the core deal. The bookmaker offers a bigger price precisely because you are carrying the non-runner risk on top of the usual risk of losing the race. In Irish National Hunt racing, where a horse's spring target can change three times between Christmas and March, that risk is real: trainers switch between the Champion Hurdle, the Stayers' and festival handicaps for sound racing reasons that have nothing to do with your docket.

What NRNB changes

Non-Runner No Bet means exactly what it says: if the horse does not run in the named race, your stake is refunded (or voided before settlement). You keep the ability to lock in a price weeks ahead without the all-in risk. The catch — and there is always a catch — is the price. When a book switches a race to NRNB, it typically trims the odds at the same time, because it has given away the non-runner margin it was previously pocketing.

When books switch it on

NRNB is a promotional lever, so it appears when festivals get close enough for the market to heat up. For Cheltenham, the major firms usually go NRNB on the championship races somewhere between late January and mid-February, some earlier as an acquisition tactic. The same pattern repeats for Punchestown, Fairyhouse's Irish Grand National and Galway's big handicaps, though usually closer to the race. There is no rule and no fixed date — each firm decides race by race, and terms can differ between races at the same meeting.

The honest maths of the trade-off

Think of it as buying insurance. Suppose a horse is 10/1 all-in but 8/1 NRNB. If you judge the chance of the horse actually running at, say, 80%, the all-in price is effectively 10/1 × 0.8 of the time and a lost stake the rest — the expected return on a €10 stake is about €88, versus €90 from the 8/1 NRNB price with the stake safe on a non-run. On those numbers the NRNB price is the better deal; flip the probability to 95% a runner and the all-in 10/1 wins. The skill is not picking the badge on the offer, it is estimating how likely the horse is to turn up.

Details worth checking before you strike it

Read where the refund goes: cash back to balance is standard, but some promotions refund as a free bet, which is worth less than cash because the stake is not returned on a winner. Check whether NRNB applies from the moment of the offer or only to bets placed after the switch — earlier ante-post bets usually stay all-in. And remember that under Rule 4-style deductions and reformed markets, a late non-runner on the day is a different mechanism again: day-of-race bets are not ante-post and are voided on non-runners as a matter of course.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Non-Runner No Bet mean?

Your stake is refunded if your selection does not run in the named race. It removes the all-in risk of standard ante-post betting — usually in exchange for a shorter price than the all-in market.

When do bookmakers go NRNB for Cheltenham?

There is no fixed date. Most major firms switch the championship races to NRNB between late January and mid-February, with some going earlier as a marketing tactic. Terms vary by firm and even by race at the same meeting.

Is NRNB better than ante-post all-in?

Neither is automatically better. NRNB is insurance paid for through a shorter price. If the horse is very likely to run, the bigger all-in price is often the better value; if its spring target is genuinely uncertain, NRNB usually wins the maths.

Ante-Post Betting Ireland: Early Prices, NRNB and the Real Risks

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

Ante-post betting — backing a horse for a big race weeks or months in advance — is the purest trade in racing: you accept the risk that your horse never runs in exchange for a bigger price than you will get on the day. Our racing guide covers the basics; this page goes deeper into when that trade is worth making, and when it is a donation. The defining rule first: in standard ante-post terms, if your horse does not run, you lose your stake. Everything below flows from that.

The maths of the no-run risk

An ante-post price has to beat the day-of-race price by enough to cover the chance of a non-runner. Honest arithmetic: 16/1 now versus a likely 10/1 on the day is only value if the horse is better than about 65% likely to actually line up — and months out, with entries, training setbacks, ground preferences and target changes all unresolved, many horses are not. The question is never “is this price big?” but “is this price big enough to pay for the months of things that can go wrong?”

The seasonal value windows

Ante-post value is seasonal because information arrives in bursts. For the spring festivals, the autumn months offer the biggest prices and the biggest uncertainty; prices tighten sharply each time a horse wins a recognised trial through the winter. The honest pattern: the best ante-post bets are struck just before a horse’s intentions become obvious to everyone — after a quiet reappearance win, before connections confirm the target. Betting after the trial everyone watched means taking a price the whole market has already corrected. Early season, the edge is judging which horses will even turn up; late season, there is usually little edge left at all.

NRNB: when the trade changes

Non-runner no bet (NRNB) refunds your stake if the horse does not run — which deletes the defining ante-post risk. Bookmakers switch big races to NRNB in the weeks before the festival, at prices shorter than the standard ante-post book. The timing question is therefore a three-way choice: big price with full no-run risk early, shorter price with no risk once NRNB arrives, or the day-of-race market. A sensible habit: treat the arrival of NRNB as a deadline for your thinking — any horse you fancied but did not trust to run becomes backable the day NRNB starts, and holding out for the early price after that point is risk without reward.

Stable switches, ballots and the fine print

Standard ante-post rules contain traps beyond the non-runner. A horse sold and moved to a new yard may void some bookmakers’ ante-post bets and not others; a race abandoned or rescheduled has its own rules; and in big handicaps a horse can be balloted out — eliminated because too many entries stand their ground — which settles as a loser under standard terms through no fault of the horse. Read the ante-post rules of the bookmaker you use before striking a months-out bet; the differences between firms are real and only ever matter after something has gone wrong.

Portfolio thinking

Serious ante-post punters think in books, not bets: several positions for one festival, sized so that the inevitable non-runners are paid for by the one or two that land at a price. That only works if the prices taken were genuinely bigger than the day-of-race market would offer — which is the discipline in a sentence. Record every ante-post bet against the eventual starting price; if you are not consistently beating it, the early market is beating you.

The honest summary

Ante-post betting is a trade in run-risk: price it like one. Take early prices only when they are big enough to pay for the uncertainty, treat NRNB as the moment the game changes, read the fine print on switches and ballots, and audit yourself against SP. Frequently Asked Questions

What happens to an ante-post bet if the horse does not run?

Under standard ante-post terms you lose your stake — that is the core risk you are paid a bigger price to accept. Once a bookmaker switches the race to non-runner no bet (NRNB), stakes on non-runners are refunded instead, at the cost of shorter prices.

When is the best time to place ante-post bets?

Just before a horse’s target becomes obvious to the whole market — typically after a quiet early-season win but before connections confirm plans or the horse lands the trial everyone watches. After that, prices correct quickly and the early-price edge is gone.

What does balloted out mean in big handicaps?

When too many entries stand their ground for a handicap, lower-rated horses are eliminated — balloted out — at the final declaration stages. Under standard ante-post terms such a bet is settled as a loser, which is one more reason to read each bookmaker’s ante-post rules.

Non-Runner No-Bet (NRNB): What It Means for Ante-Post

Standard ante-post rules cost you your stake if the horse does not run. Under NRNB terms, your stake is refunded when a declared runner is subsequently withdrawn — giving you much of the security of a race-day bet while keeping some of the early-price premium.

NRNB is widely available on the Grand National and increasingly on Cheltenham. The trade-off is a shorter price. As a rule of thumb, prices on NRNB markets are 10–25% shorter than the same horse under standard ante-post rules, depending on how likely a non-run is perceived to be.

For Grand National ante-post: watch the BHA weights publication in February. Horses at or near the bottom of the weights are most at risk of ballot if the field is oversubscribed and are also at risk of losing their mark if they race beforehand.

Best-Odds Guaranteed (BOG) and Ante-Post Bets

BOG applies on race-day bets, not ante-post bets. If you place a price boost ante-post and the SP is bigger on the day, you receive your ante-post price — the SP is irrelevant. This cuts both ways: a drifter on the day produces no uplift. For short-priced, in-form horses heading to festivals where the SP often shortens, ante-post is typically the better play. For less certain runners in open races, taking SP at the race removes that risk.

What Is Ante Post Betting: Rules, Risks and Best Practices

Ante post betting means placing a wager on a race or event weeks or months before it takes place, at odds available now rather than on the day. The main trade-off: you usually get better odds, but you lose your stake if your selection does not run (unless the bookmaker offers non-runner no-bet terms). In Ireland, ante post markets are most popular for Cheltenham, the Grand National, the Irish National and the Punchestown Festival. Cheltenham Champion Hurdle 2027 Tips Ireland

The Champion Hurdle is the feature race of Champion Day (Tuesday). Run over 2 miles, it suits sharp, speedy hurdlers rather than stayers. Irish runners have won the Champion Hurdle regularly. When assessing ante-post tips, check the horse's Grade 1 form at Leopardstown (Christmas and Dublin Racing Festival) — these are the key form guides for Cheltenham Champion Hurdle contenders.

Grand National 2027 Each Way Tips Ireland

The Grand National at Aintree in April 2027 is the most bet-on horse race in Ireland. Each-way terms are typically 1/4 odds, first 5 or 6 places depending on the operator. With 40 runners and extreme conditions, each-way value lies in horses priced 20/1–50/1 with proven jumping ability and a previous National or quality chase win. Ante-post each-way bets should be placed with NRNB operators to protect against late scratchings.

Irish Grand National 2027 Tips Ireland

The Irish Grand National runs at Fairyhouse on Easter Monday. It is a handicap chase over 3½ miles and is Ireland's most popular domestic National Hunt race for betting. Each-way terms: 1/4 odds, 1–4 places (ante-post) or 1–5 places (day of race). Irish trainers dominate — look for Willie Mullins and Gordon Elliott entries announced in March.

Punchestown Festival 2027 Betting Tips Ireland

Punchestown in late April/early May is the culmination of the Irish National Hunt season. Many Cheltenham winners run again at Punchestown, often in similar Grade 1 contests. Ante-post markets open after Cheltenham. The Champion Chase, Gold Cup equivalent (Punchestown Gold Cup), and Champion Hurdle rematch are the key betting races. BOG is widely available from Irish operators for Punchestown.

Leopardstown Christmas Festival 2027 Tips Ireland

Leopardstown Christmas Festival (26–29 December) is the busiest betting period of the Irish horse racing year. The Irish Gold Cup, Leopardstown Chase, and Deloitte Novice Hurdle are the marquee races. Many Gold Cup and Champion Hurdle contenders run here as a Cheltenham prep. Prices are often generous 10–12 weeks before Cheltenham, making Christmas Leopardstown the best ante-post window of the year for Irish punters.

Galway Races 2027 Betting Tips Ireland

The Galway Festival (late July/early August) is the biggest flat and mixed meeting in Ireland by betting volume. The Galway Plate (handicap chase) and Galway Hurdle (handicap hurdle) are the most heavily bet races. High-draw bias and competitive handicaps make Galway notoriously hard to solve — focus on market movers on race morning and horses proven at the track.

Cheltenham Festival 2027: Tips Ireland Best Bets

Cheltenham 2027 runs from Tuesday 9 March to Friday 12 March. Irish-trained horses have dominated the festival in recent years, with Willie Mullins and Gordon Elliott leading the Irish charge. The best ante-post value for Irish punters typically emerges in October-November when prices are at their longest -- markets for the Gold Cup, Champion Hurdle, and Stayers Hurdle open most of a year before the festival.

Cheltenham Gold Cup 2027: Ante Post Tips Ireland

The Cheltenham Gold Cup is the blue riband of jump racing, run on Gold Cup Friday (Day 4). Irish-trained horses have won the Gold Cup in the majority of recent years. Ante-post betting opens from early in the season -- October/November -- with early prices often the longest you will see. NRNB protection on ante-post Gold Cup bets is essential given the long campaign ahead for horses and the risk of injury before March.

Best Each Way Bets Cheltenham 2027 Ireland

Each-way value at Cheltenham comes in the big handicaps with 15-20+ runners where place terms of 4 places at 1/4 odds apply. Races to target for EW betting: County Hurdle (20+ runners, 4 places), Coral Cup (25+ runners, 4 places), Pertemps Final (20+ runners), Martin Pipe Conditional Jockeys Handicap Hurdle (20+ runners). Irish-trained outsiders at 16/1-33/1 ridden by top jockeys are the classic Cheltenham each-way play.

Willie Mullins Cheltenham 2027 Runners

Willie Mullins is Ireland's champion National Hunt trainer and typically sends the largest Irish team to Cheltenham, with runners in almost every race. His strength lies in novice hurdle and novice chase divisions, where he has dominated in recent years. Ante-post markets for Mullins runners are usually available from October -- check racing media (Racing Post, At The Races, Racing TV) for the latest Mullins entry lists as the season progresses. No confirmed entry list is available until the January forfeit stages.

Gordon Elliott Cheltenham 2027 Entries

Gordon Elliott is the other leading Irish raider at Cheltenham. Elliott tends to target the staying chases and handicap races alongside the novice divisions. His runners typically represent excellent each-way value in the big handicaps. Entry lists are published after the January and February forfeit deadlines -- follow Racing Post for updates.

Nicky Henderson Cheltenham 2027 Horses

Nicky Henderson is the leading British trainer at Cheltenham, based at Seven Barrows in Lambourn. Henderson is champion trainer at the festival more times than any other, with his strength in the Champion Hurdle and Supreme Novices Hurdle divisions. British ante-post punters should track his declarations from January. His stable form in January-February is a reliable Cheltenham indicator.

Ante Post Betting Ireland: What It Means and Key Rules

Ante post betting in Ireland means placing a bet on a horse race before the day of the event, often weeks or months in advance. Irish punters use ante post markets heavily for Cheltenham, the Punchestown Festival, and the Irish Grand National. The main risk is that your stake is lost if your horse does not run, unless Non-Runner No Bet (NRNB) protection applies.

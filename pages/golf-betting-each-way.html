Golf Betting & Each-Way Places

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

Golf is the sport the each-way bet was made for. A standard tour event sends well over a hundred players out on Thursday, and even the best player in the field wins far less often than favourites do in two-horse sports. Outright prices are therefore huge — and almost nobody who bets golf seriously backs win-only. This guide covers the place terms, and the dead-heat rule that quietly reshapes golf payouts more than in any other sport.

Why win-only rarely makes sense in golf

Over 72 holes, variance is enormous: weather draws split the field, one bad nine ends a week, and a putter running cold undoes four days of good ball-striking. Even elite players convert only a small fraction of their starts into wins. The each-way bet splits your stake — half on the win, half on a place — so a player who contends without winning still returns something. In a sport where “contending” is the realistic good outcome, that second half of the bet is the whole strategy.

Place terms: 5 places is standard, 8–10 is a promotion

The standard golf each-way offer is around a quarter (1/4) or a fifth (1/5) of the odds for a top-5 finish. Around the biggest events, bookmakers compete by extending places — six, eight, sometimes ten. More places means more ways to collect, but read the whole offer: extended places often come with a smaller odds fraction, and the honest comparison is the place price you are actually getting. A fifth of the odds for 8 places can beat a quarter for 5 for a steady contender, and the reverse for a boom-or-bust pick. Do the division before being dazzled by the place count.

Dead heats: where golf bites hardest

Golf produces ties constantly — five players sharing fourth place is an ordinary Sunday. Bookmakers settle ties on place bets with the dead-heat reduction: your stake is divided by the number of players tied for the remaining places.

Worked example, house style. You have €5 each-way at 40/1, 1/5 odds for 5 places (€10 total). Your player ties for 5th with three others — four players, one remaining place. The place part settles as if you had staked €5 × ¼ = €1.25 at the place price of 8/1 (a fifth of 40/1): €1.25 × 9 = €11.25 back. Without the tie you would have collected €45. Same finishing position on the leaderboard, a quarter of the payout — that is the dead-heat rule, it is standard across the industry, and no one shows it to you in the advert.

The Irish angle

Irish interest in golf betting peaks around the majors and the Ryder Cup, and whenever Irish players are in contention the prices on them tighten with patriotic money. That is worth saying plainly: the market charges extra for backing the home favourite. The Irish Open is the week this effect is strongest. Sentiment is a fine reason to watch; it is an expensive reason to bet.

The honest summary

Golf betting is each-way betting: compare the real place price (fraction × odds × places), expect dead-heat reductions as routine rather than bad luck, and remember that half your stake is riding on an outcome — the win — that rarely arrives even for the best in the world.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does an each-way golf bet work?

Your stake is split in two: half on the player to win, half on them to finish inside the place terms (for example top 5 at 1/5 odds). If the player places without winning, the win half loses and the place half pays — which in golf is the realistic good outcome.

What is a dead-heat reduction in golf betting?

When players tie for the last paying places, your place stake is divided in proportion to ties and places remaining. Four players tied for one remaining place → the place part settles on a quarter of your stake at the full place odds. Ties are routine in golf, so this rule affects golf payouts more than any other sport’s.

Are extra each-way places always better value?

Not automatically. Extended places sometimes come with a smaller fraction of the odds, so compare the actual place price — fraction × odds across the places offered. Eight places at 1/5 can be better or worse than five at 1/4 depending on whether your pick is a steady contender or a boom-or-bust type.

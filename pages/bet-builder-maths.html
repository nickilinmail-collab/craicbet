Bet Builder Maths: What Same-Game Combos Really Cost

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

Bet builders — same-game combos stitching together a result, a scorer, cards and corners — are the fastest-growing product in football betting, and the most profitable one for bookmakers. The reason is pure arithmetic: every leg you add carries its own slice of margin, and the slices multiply. Here is the maths, so you can see the price tag before you pay it.

How a builder is priced: margin on every leg

Take three legs the bookmaker believes are genuine 50/50 propositions. At fair odds each would be 2.00, and the fair combo 2.00 × 2.00 × 2.00 = 8.00. But each leg is offered at around 1.87 once a typical single-market margin is baked in. Your builder pays 1.87 × 1.87 × 1.87 ≈ 6.54. You are being paid 6.54 for an 8.00 shot — roughly an 18% haircut, versus about 6–7% on any one leg alone. The margin doesn’t add; it compounds.

Correlation: the quiet repricing

Legs are rarely independent. “Team to win” and “their striker to score” are positively correlated — if one happens, the other is more likely. A naive multiplication would overpay you, so the pricing engine reprices correlated combinations downward, often sharply. That is why a builder’s quoted odds are usually well below the product of the individual prices you can see on the main market. The engine never reprices in your favour; combinations it can’t model safely are simply blocked.

A worked example of stacking

Suppose a builder quotes 11.0 for a four-leg combo whose legs, priced as singles, multiply to 15.2. The gap is the combined cost of per-leg margin plus the correlation adjustment. To find your break-even: you need the combo to land more than 1 in 11 times (9.1%) just to stand still, while the bookmaker’s own numbers imply it lands nearer 1 in 15 (6.6%). That spread is the product’s entire business model.

When builders are fair-ish

The haircut is smallest when legs are truly independent — say, outcomes from different phases of the game with little causal link — and when each leg sits in a liquid, low-margin market like match result or over/under 2.5 goals. Two-leg builders in main markets can cost only a little more than an ordinary double. The damage escalates with leg count and with exotic legs (player cards, exact corners brackets), where single-market margins are already wide before compounding starts.

Cash out makes it worse, not better

Cash out on a builder is priced off the same engine, with another margin applied at the moment you accept. Cashing out a builder that is three legs up is routinely the worst-value click in the app: you crystallise the compounded margin and pay a fresh one on exit.

Honest rules for Irish punters

If you enjoy builders, treat them as entertainment with a known cost: keep legs to two or three, stay in main markets, compare the quoted combo against the multiplied singles to see the haircut, and never stake more on a builder than you would on a single. Frequently Asked Questions

Why are my bet builder odds lower than multiplying the single prices?

Two reasons: each leg carries its own margin, and correlated legs (team win + their striker to score) are repriced downward because a naive multiplication would overpay. The engine only ever adjusts against you.

Are two-leg bet builders better value than big ones?

Generally yes. Margin compounds with every leg, so a two-leg builder in liquid main markets costs only a little more than an ordinary double. Six or seven legs with player props and corners brackets can push the combined haircut to several times that.

Is cashing out a bet builder good value?

Usually not. The cash-out figure comes from the same pricing engine that built the compounded margin in, and a fresh margin is applied at the moment you accept — so you pay the haircut twice.

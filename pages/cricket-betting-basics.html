Cricket Betting Basics: Three Formats, Three Different Games

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

Cricket has quietly grown an Irish audience — Ireland has played Test cricket since 2018, the national side meets the biggest teams in world cricket across formats, and a large cricket-following community here keeps the betting markets busy. But cricket punishes punters who treat it as one sport. It is three: Tests, one-dayers and T20 each have their own markets, their own maths and their own traps. This guide covers the basics honestly.

Match odds: mind the draw

In limited-overs cricket the match-odds market is two-way (ties are rare and usually settled by super overs or dead-heat rules). Test cricket is different: the draw is a third, frequently landing outcome. Five days of weather, flat pitches and time running out mean “neither side wins” is often a genuine favourite by day four. A Test match-odds bet without a view on the draw is half a bet — and backing a side that needs to force a result against both the opponent and the clock is a harder task than the price sometimes admits.

Innings runs and session markets

Innings-runs lines (over/under a total for a team’s innings) are cricket’s core numbers market, and they are driven by conditions as much as by batting: pitch character, ground size, an evening dew or overhead cloud can move a fair line by dozens of runs. The honest approach is to form a conditions view before looking at the line — if you only react to the number, you are guessing against a bookmaker who has already priced the pitch report.

DLS: the settlement trap

Rain-affected limited-overs matches are decided by the DLS method — a recalculated target based on overs lost and wickets in hand. Two consequences for punters. First, the result itself can swing on the recalculation: a side comfortably ahead on runs can be behind on DLS the moment it rains. Second, settlement: innings totals, player runs and most props have specific reduced-overs rules — commonly a minimum number of overs for bets to stand, otherwise void. Before betting with weather around, read the DLS and abandonment rules; confusion here is the single most common cricket betting complaint.

T20: volatility priced as certainty

Twenty overs is a tiny sample. One over of hitting changes a T20 match price by huge swings, and favourites lose constantly — which is the format’s charm and its danger. Short pre-match favourites in T20 carry football-sized prices with far more variance behind them, and in-play prices lurch ball by ball. The honest rule: stake smaller in T20 than the same confidence would justify in a Test or ODI, because the format itself is a variance multiplier.

Player markets and the margin

Top batter, top bowler and player-runs lines are popular and fun, but they are wide-field or prop markets with margins to match — the bookmaker’s cut on a top-batter market is much larger than on match odds. Treat them as small-stakes entertainment, and watch the settlement small print: top-batter markets usually apply dead-heat rules when scores tie, which quietly shrinks winning payouts.

The honest summary

Respect the draw in Tests, form a conditions view before betting any runs line, read the DLS rules whenever clouds appear, and halve your T20 confidence. Cricket rewards patience and punishes punters who bring one format’s instincts to another.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the draw so important in Test match betting?

Because it is a third outcome that lands frequently: weather, flat pitches and the five-day clock mean neither side wins many Tests. By late in a match the draw can be favourite, and any match-odds bet needs a view on it, not just on the two teams.

What is DLS and how does it affect my bet?

The DLS method recalculates targets in rain-affected limited-overs games based on overs lost and wickets in hand. It can flip who is ahead, and it triggers special settlement rules: many markets need a minimum number of overs to stand, otherwise bets are void. Check the rules before betting under grey skies.

Is T20 harder to bet on than Test cricket?

It is higher-variance: twenty overs is a tiny sample, one big over reshapes the match, and favourites lose far more often than equivalent prices in longer formats. The sensible response is smaller stakes for the same level of confidence.

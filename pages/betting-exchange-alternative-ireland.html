What Is a Betting Exchange?

A betting exchange is fundamentally different in structure from a traditional bookmaker. Rather than betting against a bookmaker who sets the odds, exchange users bet against each other. The exchange platform matches “backers” (who bet that an outcome will happen) with “layers” (who bet that it will not). The exchange itself takes no position on events — it simply facilitates the market and charges a commission on net winnings.

The two dominant betting exchanges in the Irish market are Betfair and Betdaq. Betfair, launched in 2000, is the largest betting exchange in the world by liquidity and is owned by Flutter Entertainment, the Irish-rooted gambling giant that also owns Paddy Power and Sky Bet. Betdaq, now owned by Entain (the group behind Ladbrokes and Coral), is a smaller exchange that competes on lower commission rates, particularly for horse racing.

Both exchanges have been active in Ireland for over two decades and are well-established in the Irish betting market, particularly among horse racing enthusiasts and professional or semi-professional bettors who trade markets.

How Exchange Odds Differ from Bookmaker Odds

Traditional bookmakers build a margin — often called the overround or vig — into their odds. If you add up the implied probabilities of all outcomes in a market based on the bookmaker’s odds, they sum to more than 100%. That excess represents the bookmaker’s expected margin. For a typical football match, bookmaker margins on the 1X2 market (home win, draw, away win) are often in the range of 5–10%, meaning you are effectively paying a 5–10% tax on each bet in the form of reduced odds.

Betting exchange odds are set by the market participants themselves and in liquid markets tend to be much closer to the true probability of outcomes. The exchange’s revenue comes from charging commission — typically 2–5% of net winnings on a market, deducted after any successful bet — rather than from building a margin into the odds.

For high-volume bettors, this structural difference can be significant. At a 5% bookmaker margin versus a 2% exchange commission, the exchange is materially cheaper for a bettor who places large volumes and wins consistently. For casual bettors placing small stakes infrequently, the difference in real terms may be modest.

Lay Betting: What It Is and Why It Matters

Lay betting is the defining feature of a betting exchange that no traditional bookmaker can replicate. When you lay an outcome, you are betting that it will not happen. You are, in effect, acting as the bookmaker for that bet:

  • If the outcome you laid does not occur, you collect the backer’s stake (minus commission) as your profit.
  • If the outcome does occur, you pay out at the agreed odds multiplied by the backer’s stake.

The liability when laying can be substantial. If you lay a horse at odds of 10/1 for a €10 stake, your maximum liability is €100 (€10 × 10) if the horse wins. This is why lay betting requires careful bankroll management and is typically used by experienced bettors.

Lay betting is used for several purposes in the Irish market:

  • Matched betting: Using a free bet offer from a traditional bookmaker and laying the same selection on an exchange to remove risk from the bonus, locking in a guaranteed profit regardless of the outcome. This is a legal and widely practised technique.
  • Trading: Backing a selection before an event and laying it at shorter odds (after a price movement) to lock in a profit or reduce risk before the event concludes. Common in horse racing markets where prices move in the period before a race.
  • Dutching and hedging: Using lay bets to cover multiple outcomes simultaneously or to hedge a position in a traditional bookmaker account.

None of these strategies are available at a traditional bookmaker. If lay betting or exchange trading is part of your approach, an exchange — with its GRAI-licensed status confirmed at grai.ie — is the product you need. A traditional bookmaker cannot substitute for it.

When a GRAI-Licensed Bookmaker Is a Better Fit

Exchanges are powerful tools, but they are not always the right choice. GRAI-licensed traditional bookmakers offer genuine advantages in several scenarios:

Smaller stakes and casual bettors. Exchange commission on small stakes can feel disproportionate and the interface is more complex than a standard bookmaker. For casual bettors placing €5–€20 bets a few times per week, a traditional bookmaker is simpler and may offer comparable value after accounting for welcome bonuses and ongoing promotions.

Welcome bonuses. Betting exchanges do not typically offer welcome bonuses in the same form as traditional bookmakers. A GRAI-licensed bookmaker’s welcome offer — if the terms are reasonable — can provide genuinely better initial expected value for a new bettor than starting with an exchange.

Fixed-odds certainty. Traditional bookmakers offer fixed odds: the price you see when you place your bet is the price you get (barring SP bets). On an exchange, your bet must be matched by another user. In low-liquidity markets, your bet may not be fully matched, particularly pre-event. If you need certainty that your bet will be placed, a bookmaker is more reliable for thin markets.

Non-exchange markets. Exchanges focus on the most liquid markets. For obscure events, local leagues, or GAA club championship matches, traditional bookmakers may offer markets that simply do not exist on an exchange due to insufficient liquidity.

Enhanced odds and special offers. Traditional bookmakers regularly offer price boosts, early prices on horse racing with best odds guaranteed (BOG), and in-play specials that have no exchange equivalent. For bettors who value these offers, a GRAI-licensed bookmaker is the right tool.

GRAI Licensing for Exchanges and Bookmakers in Ireland

Since 1 July 2026, any operator targeting Irish customers with betting products must hold a GRAI remote betting licence. This requirement applies equally to betting exchanges and traditional bookmakers. The GRAI licensing framework does not distinguish between exchange and bookmaker business models — both need a GRAI remote betting licence.

Before using any exchange or bookmaker in Ireland, check the GRAI public register at grai.ie to confirm that the specific operator holds a current GRAI licence. Each valid licence carries a reference number in the format GRAI-XXXX-RB-26-XXXX.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a betting exchange and how does it differ from a traditional bookmaker?▾
A betting exchange is a platform where bettors bet against each other rather than against the bookmaker. The exchange itself does not set odds or take positions — it simply facilitates matched bets between users who want to back (bet for) or lay (bet against) an outcome. The exchange makes money by charging commission on net winnings, typically 2–5%. A traditional bookmaker sets its own odds, takes your bet against its own book, and profits from the margin built into those odds. Exchanges typically offer better odds but charge commission; traditional bookmakers offer no commission but build their margin into the odds.
What is lay betting and why do bettors use exchanges for it?▾
Lay betting is betting against an outcome — effectively taking the role of the bookmaker. On a betting exchange, you can lay a horse to lose, lay a football team to fail to win, or lay any other outcome. If the outcome you laid does not occur, you win the backer’s stake (minus commission). If it does occur, you pay out at the agreed odds. Lay betting is only available on exchanges like Betfair or Betdaq — traditional bookmakers do not offer it. It is used by matched bettors, traders, and experienced bettors seeking to hedge positions.
Do Betfair and Betdaq hold GRAI licences in Ireland?▾
You should always verify current licence status directly at grai.ie. Both Betfair (owned by Flutter Entertainment) and Betdaq (owned by Entain) have been major operators in Ireland for many years and are likely applicants for GRAI licensing. Check the GRAI public register for the current status of any specific operator before depositing.
What is the commission rate on betting exchanges and how does it compare to bookmaker margins?▾
Betting exchanges typically charge 2–5% commission on net winnings (commission is usually only charged on winning markets, not on each individual bet). Traditional bookmakers build a margin of typically 5–10% into their odds. For high-volume bettors, exchanges can be significantly cheaper. For casual bettors who place small volumes, the difference may be less material. A GRAI-licensed bookmaker with competitive odds may still offer comparable value on mainstream markets.
Can I find a GRAI-licensed alternative to betting exchanges in Ireland?▾
GRAI-licensed traditional bookmakers offer an alternative for bettors whose primary need is competitive odds and wide market coverage, but they do not offer lay betting or peer-to-peer market trading.

Double Chance: Two Results, One Price — and One Trap

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 1 November 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

Double chance lets you back two of the three football outcomes in one bet: 1X (home win or draw), X2 (draw or away win), 12 (either team wins — no draw). Covering two results out of three sounds like the sensible-punter’s market, and sometimes it is. But double chance has a specific trap, and it is not in the rules — it is in what short prices do to accumulators.

How the price is made

A double chance price is just the two underlying 1X2 outcomes fused. You can replicate it yourself: back both outcomes on the 1X2 with stakes sized so either result returns the same amount, and compare that blended return with the listed double chance price. Sometimes the DIY split pays slightly better, sometimes the listed market does — books know most customers never check. The deeper point stands for every “convenience” market: it is built from parts you can price yourself, and checking takes less than a minute.

1X, X2, 12: what each is for

1X backs a favourite with draw cover, or an underdog at home to avoid defeat. X2 is the classic away-value play: a decent side away from home where the draw is live. 12 is the odd one out — it bets against the draw, useful in matches where both teams must attack, and it is the mirror image of backing the draw itself. Each only earns its place when the second outcome you are covering is genuinely probable; covering a 5% draw costs more in price than it returns in safety.

Double chance vs draw no bet

1X and DNB cover the same fear differently: DNB refunds on the draw, 1X wins on it — so 1X is always the shorter price of the two. The ladder from brave to cautious runs: straight win, draw no bet, double chance. The right rung depends on your real opinion of the draw. If you find yourself always buying maximum cover regardless of the match, you are not managing risk, you are paying a recurring comfort fee — and over a season those fees are exactly how the margin eats a careful punter.

The wrong comfort blanket: short prices in accas

Here is the trap. Double chance prices on favourites are short — often in the 1.10 to 1.30 range — and short prices feel safe, so they get stacked into accumulators as “bankers”. Stack six legs at 1.20 and the acca pays about 2.99 total odds — under 2/1 for needing six results to go right. Meanwhile each leg carries its own slice of margin, and multiplying six slices together builds the house a far bigger cut than any single bet pays. A long acca of heavy favourites is the most expensive way to bet small opinions: the headline feels safe, the compounding is doing the opposite.

When double chance is the right call

As a single, with the draw genuinely live, at a price you have checked against the DIY 1X2 split — double chance is a perfectly honest product. Cup ties with a strong underdog at home, derby matches where draws are historically common, an away side that rarely loses but often draws: these are 1X and X2 territory on merit, not comfort.

The honest summary

Double chance is two-thirds of the 1X2 at a blended price you can verify yourself. Use it as a single when the covered outcome is truly probable, compare it against draw no bet before paying for more cover than you need, and keep it out of long accas — stacked short prices are where the comfort quietly becomes the cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a 1X double chance bet mean?

1X wins on a home win or a draw — it only loses if the away side wins. The other two options: X2 covers draw-or-away, and 12 covers either team winning (it loses only on the draw). You are backing two of the three 1X2 outcomes in one bet.

Is double chance the same as draw no bet?

No. On a draw, 1X double chance pays as a winner while draw no bet only refunds the stake — so 1X always carries the shorter price of the two. Pick between them based on how likely you think the draw really is, not on which feels safer.

Why are double chance accas poor value?

Each leg looks safe, but each carries its own slice of bookmaker margin and the slices multiply. Six legs at 1.20 pay roughly 2.99 total — under 2/1 for six correct results — while compounding six lots of house edge. The headline feels like a banker; the maths is a recurring fee.

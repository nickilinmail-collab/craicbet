Unlicensed Betting Sites in Ireland: What You Need to Know

Last updated: 5 November 2026

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 5 November 2026 ⏱ 8 min read

On 1 July 2026, Ireland's gambling landscape changed permanently. Under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, every online bookmaker that accepts bets from Irish residents must now hold a valid licence from the Gambling Regulatory Authority of Ireland (GRAI). Any operator doing business without that licence is committing a criminal offence. This page explains what that means for Irish bettors — the risks of unlicensed sites, how to identify them, and what to do if you encounter one.

The Legal Position Since 1 July 2026

Before July 2026, online bookmakers operating in Ireland needed only a relatively straightforward Revenue Commissioner permission — a system designed for in-person bookmakers in the 1930s and never rebuilt for the internet age. That framework ended on 30 June 2026.

From 1 July 2026, every remote betting operator must hold a GRAI remote betting licence to legally accept bets from anyone in Ireland. The requirement applies regardless of where the operator is headquartered. A bookmaker based in Malta, Gibraltar, the Isle of Man, or anywhere else in the world must hold a GRAI licence if it accepts bets from Irish customers. Domestic location is irrelevant to the legal obligation.

As of September 2026, the GRAI had issued 32 remote betting licences. Any operator not on that list and still accepting Irish customers is in breach of the Gambling Regulation Act 2024.

⚠ Important: The legal risk for operating without a GRAI licence falls on the operator, not the player. It is not currently a criminal offence for an individual to place a bet on an unlicensed site. However, the practical and financial risk falls entirely on you as the customer. This is what this guide covers.

Penalties for Unlicensed Operators

The Gambling Regulation Act 2024 introduced some of the most substantial penalties in European gambling law for operating without a licence. Unlicensed operators face:

  • Fines of up to €20 million or 10% of annual global turnover — whichever figure is higher
  • Criminal prosecution for directors and officers, with sentences of up to five years' imprisonment
  • Court-ordered injunctions to block access to the site within Ireland (internet blocking)
  • Asset freezing and seizure orders in the most serious cases

The severity of these penalties reflects how seriously the Irish legislature took the transition to regulated online gambling. For context, the GRAI's enforcement powers are substantially stronger than those of the previous Revenue Commissioner framework, which had limited enforcement tools against offshore operators.

Risks for Bettors Using Unlicensed Sites

The criminal liability rests with the operator. But the practical consequences of using an unlicensed site affect you directly — often in ways that only become apparent when something goes wrong.

No Legal Obligation to Pay Winnings

GRAI-licensed bookmakers are required by their licence conditions to pay out legitimate winnings. If they refuse, you can escalate to the GRAI, which can investigate, sanction, and ultimately revoke the operator's licence. Paying winnings is a licence condition under Irish law, not just a contractual promise.

With an unlicensed offshore site, payout is a matter of foreign contract law. If the site disputes your withdrawal, your practical options are: (a) complaining to a foreign regulator who may have limited appetite to act against an operator in another jurisdiction; (b) civil litigation in a foreign court, which is expensive and slow; or (c) accepting the loss. For most bettors, option (c) is the realistic outcome.

No Access to Ireland's Self-Exclusion Register

The GRAI is building a National Gambling Exclusion Register, which will allow any Irish gambler to self-exclude from all GRAI-licensed operators simultaneously with a single registration. GRAI-licensed operators are legally required to check this register before opening any new account.

Unlicensed sites are not connected to this register and are under no obligation to participate. If you have self-excluded from licensed sites but continue using an unlicensed site, the self-exclusion has no effect there. For anyone managing a gambling problem, this is a significant practical risk.

No Formal Complaints Route

GRAI-licensed bookmakers must maintain an internal complaints process, and if that fails, customers can escalate directly to the GRAI as the national regulator. This is a meaningful remedy — the GRAI can sanction an operator that repeatedly fails to handle complaints properly.

Unlicensed sites typically offer a complaints email address and nothing else. If the site dismisses your complaint, your escalation options are limited to a foreign regulator (Malta Gaming Authority, Gibraltar Regulatory Authority, etc.) who may not prioritise individual consumer disputes.

No Deposit Limit Enforcement

GRAI rules require licensed operators to offer daily, weekly, and monthly deposit limits, with any request to reduce a limit taking effect immediately and any request to increase a limit subject to a mandatory cooling-off period. These are legal requirements, not optional features.

Unlicensed sites may offer deposit limit tools voluntarily, but there is no enforcement mechanism. Many offshore sites do not offer them at all, or offer them in a way that makes limits easy to remove without delay. For anyone who relies on deposit limits as a responsible gambling tool, an unlicensed site provides no guarantee.

No Protection If the Site Closes

GRAI licence conditions include requirements around segregation of customer funds and financial stability. These conditions exist precisely to ensure that if an operator experiences financial difficulty, your balance is protected. Unlicensed offshore operators have no equivalent Irish requirement. If an unlicensed site closes without warning — which offshore sites occasionally do — your deposited balance may simply be gone.

How Unlicensed Sites Still Reach Irish Consumers

Despite the GRAI framework, some unlicensed operators continue to target Irish bettors. They typically do so through:

Offshore Licensing

Many large gambling operators hold licences from jurisdictions such as Malta (MGA), Gibraltar, the Isle of Man, or Curaçao. These are legitimate licences within those jurisdictions, but they do not constitute authorisation to serve Irish customers under Irish law. An operator can hold a valid MGA licence and be operating illegally in Ireland simultaneously, if it has not obtained a GRAI licence.

The key distinction: a foreign licence tells you the operator meets that regulator's standards. It does not give you Irish legal protections, access to the GRAI complaints route, or any of the other rights created by the Gambling Regulation Act 2024.

VPNs and Geo-Workarounds

Some unlicensed sites rely on Irish users accessing them via VPN, or simply do not geo-block Irish IP addresses even if they are not licensed here. Some users actively seek out unlicensed sites using VPNs for access to markets or odds not available from GRAI-licensed operators.

Using a VPN to access a betting site in this way is a legal grey area in Ireland — it is not explicitly criminalised for players — but it means operating entirely outside Irish consumer protection law. We cover this in more detail below.

How to Spot an Unlicensed Site

These warning signs do not definitively prove a site is unlicensed, but they are strong indicators worth investigating further:

  • No GRAI licence number in the footer. Every GRAI-licensed operator must display its licence number. Format: GRAI-XXXX-RB-YY-NNNN. If the footer shows only an MGA, Gibraltar, or Curaçao licence number, the site is not GRAI-licensed.
  • Credit cards accepted for deposits. GRAI-licensed operators are banned from accepting credit card deposits from Irish customers. If a site accepts your credit card for gambling deposits, that is a breach of GRAI licence conditions — or the site is not licensed at all.
  • No mention of GRAI or the Gambling Regulation Act 2024. Licensed operators are required to reference their GRAI licence in regulatory disclosures.
  • No deposit limit or self-exclusion tools. GRAI-licensed operators must offer these. Their absence or inaccessibility is a red flag.
  • Responsible gambling footer links to foreign organisations only. GRAI-licensed operators must reference Irish responsible gambling resources, including Gambling Awareness Trust (1800 753 753) and gamblingcare.ie.

The definitive check is always the GRAI public register at grai.ie/register. Search for the legal company name (found in the footer) and confirm the licence is Active and the type is RB. See our step-by-step GRAI register guide for the full verification process.

VPN Use to Access Blocked or Unlicensed Sites

Using a VPN to access a betting site that is geo-blocked or not licensed in Ireland sits in a legal grey area under current Irish law. The Gambling Regulation Act 2024 creates offences for operators, not for individual players accessing unlicensed sites.

However, the practical consequences are significant:

  • Most betting operators explicitly prohibit VPN use in their terms and conditions. If detected, your account can be closed and your balance voided — with no GRAI complaints route available, since the operator is not GRAI-licensed.
  • No Irish consumer protections apply. Your only recourse is through the operator's terms and the jurisdiction of wherever that operator is based.
  • If the operator is under GRAI investigation or subject to an Irish court injunction, you may find the site disappears without warning.

CraicBet does not recommend using VPNs to access unlicensed betting sites. The risk-to-reward ratio is poor: you gain access to a site with fewer protections while giving up all the rights the GRAI framework provides.

What to Do If You Encounter an Unlicensed Site

If you believe a betting site is operating in Ireland without a GRAI licence:

  1. Verify on the GRAI register at grai.ie/register before concluding the site is unlicensed — some operators display licence numbers inconsistently.
  2. Do not deposit any money if you have not already done so.
  3. Withdraw any existing balance as quickly as possible if you already hold funds there. Document everything with screenshots.
  4. Report the site to the GRAI via grai.ie. Anonymous reports are accepted. The GRAI can investigate and take enforcement action.
  5. If you believe you have been defrauded, you can also report to An Garda Síochána and seek advice from the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to use an unlicensed betting site in Ireland?

Since 1 July 2026, it is a criminal offence for an operator to offer remote betting to Irish customers without a GRAI licence. It is not currently a criminal offence for a player to use an unlicensed site. The legal risk falls on the operator. The practical risk — no Irish legal protection if the site refuses to pay, no GRAI complaints route, no self-exclusion register access — falls entirely on you.

What are the penalties for running an unlicensed betting site in Ireland?

Under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, unlicensed operators face fines of up to €20 million or 10% of annual turnover — whichever is higher — plus criminal prosecution with sentences of up to five years' imprisonment. The GRAI can also seek court injunctions to block access to unlicensed sites within Ireland.

How do I spot an unlicensed betting site?

Key warning signs: no GRAI licence number (format GRAI-XXXX-RB-YY-NNNN) in the footer; only a Malta, Gibraltar, or Curaçao licence displayed; credit cards accepted for deposits (banned under GRAI rules); no deposit limit or self-exclusion tools; no reference to Gambling Awareness Trust or gamblingcare.ie. The definitive check is the GRAI register at grai.ie/register — search for the legal company name from the footer.

Can I use a VPN to access betting sites that are blocked in Ireland?

Using a VPN to access a site blocked or unlicensed in Ireland is a legal grey area. It is not explicitly criminalised for players, but it means you are using the site in breach of its own terms (most operators prohibit VPN use) and without any Irish consumer protection. If the site refuses to pay winnings or closes, you have no GRAI complaints route and no practical Irish remedy. We do not recommend it.

How do I report an unlicensed betting site operating in Ireland?

Report suspected unlicensed operators to the GRAI through its website at grai.ie. Reports can be submitted anonymously. The GRAI has powers to investigate, fine, prosecute, and seek court injunctions to block access to unlicensed sites. You can also report to An Garda Síochána if you believe a criminal offence is being committed.

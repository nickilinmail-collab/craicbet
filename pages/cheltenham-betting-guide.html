Cheltenham Festival Betting: Complete Guide for Irish Punters

Last updated: 4 November 2026

📝 Mark Craic, editor (8 years in gambling analytics) 📅 Updated 4 November 2026 ⏱ 7 min read

Why Cheltenham Is Different

No racing festival in the world generates the same concentration of Irish betting interest as Cheltenham in March. Four days, 28 Grade 1 and Grade 2 races, thousands of Irish punters making the trip, and Irish-trained horses typically dominating the results. The Cheltenham Festival is not just a horse racing event; for Irish punters it is the annual benchmark against which everything else is measured.

What makes Cheltenham betting genuinely different from an ordinary jump meeting is the quality of the field across every race. At a standard weekday jumps meeting, there is often a clear favourite with legitimate claim to win. At Cheltenham, the best horse in each category shows up, the market is tighter, the form is harder to read, and even short-priced favourites get beaten regularly. The festival attrition rate — favourites beaten at short prices — is historically high enough that it should form part of every Cheltenham betting plan.

The track itself is a factor. Cheltenham's undulating course with its demanding uphill finish tests horses differently from most Irish tracks. Some horses who look brilliant at home simply do not handle the unique demands of the track. Cheltenham form (how a horse has previously performed at the festival) matters more than it does at almost any other venue in the world.

The Key Races to Bet On

The festival is built around four flagship races, each on a different day: the Champion Hurdle (Day 1), the Queen Mother Champion Chase (Day 2), the Stayers' Hurdle (Day 3), and the Cheltenham Gold Cup (Day 4). These four races attract the largest betting volumes and the tightest markets.

For punters seeking value, the big handicaps deserve serious attention: the Coral Cup, the Martin Pipe Conditional Jockeys' Handicap Hurdle, the Pertemps Network Final, the Ultima Handicap Chase, and the Plate Handicap Chase. These races regularly produce double-figure winners and generous each-way terms (often 5 places on 20+ runner fields). The novelty and Grade 1 chases also offer opportunities, particularly the Supreme Novices' Hurdle and the Arkle Trophy Chase on Day 1, where ante-post markets often overrate early-season stars.

Irish punters should also track the National Hunt Chase (the amateur riders' Gold Cup equivalent) and the Ryanair Chase, which frequently suit the Irish raiding party. Identifying the Irish stables' primary targets in each category before the festival begins is part of effective Cheltenham preparation.

How to Read Cheltenham Form

Cheltenham form is the most important factor most casual punters underweight. A horse that has won or placed at the festival before — especially one that has shown ability on the particular track — has a proven compatibility that is worth more than it appears in the form figures. Conversely, horses with multiple Cheltenham runs without a placing often have a track-specific problem that is not reflected in their general form.

Key form pointers for Cheltenham: previous festival runs (did the horse handle the track?), course winners from trials meetings at Cheltenham in January and February, and Irish trials form from the main preparatory races at Leopardstown, Naas, Fairyhouse and Thurles in January and February. Irish trainers structure their campaign around specific Cheltenham targets; following the stated targets of major stables is often the most reliable preparation guide.

Going matters enormously. Cheltenham has traditionally been run on good-to-soft ground with occasional heavy patches. Horses with form on soft or heavy ground from the Irish winter campaign have a natural advantage in a wet March. Conversely, horses whose best form has come on faster ground in autumn can underperform.

Ante-Post vs Race Day Betting

Ante-post Cheltenham markets open months before the festival, with early prices available from November onwards. The appeal is clear: a horse available at 20/1 in November may be 8/1 by March. The risk is equally clear: if the horse does not run, the bet is lost with no refund in most ante-post markets.

The standard strategy for Irish punters: use ante-post for horses with a clear single target (the trainer has stated this is the festival aim, the horse is in excellent health, and the race fits perfectly), and limit each-way ante-post bets to horses where the place part provides insurance. For handicaps with uncertain entries, waiting until closer to the race or the morning of the race provides far more information at the cost of shorter odds.

Festival offers from bookmakers deserve attention in the weeks before Cheltenham. Extra-place each-way promotions, money back if second, and enhanced odds offers on specific races are genuinely competitive during festival week. Frequently Asked Questions

Why do Irish-trained horses perform so well at Cheltenham?

Irish National Hunt horses are trained over similar terrain and race distances to what they face at Cheltenham. The festival's demanding jumping tests suit horses conditioned for the Irish winter programme. Irish stables have also prioritised Cheltenham as a primary target for decades, building deep expertise in preparing horses specifically for the festival.

Is it better to bet ante-post or on the day at Cheltenham?

Ante-post betting offers better odds but no refund if the horse does not run. Race day betting offers worse prices but you only bet on confirmed runners. Best strategy: ante-post on horses with a clear single target, race-day bets for handicaps and races with uncertain runners.

What is the best race to bet at Cheltenham for value?

The big handicaps — particularly the Coral Cup, Pertemps Final and Plate Handicap Chase — often offer the best each-way value due to large fields and competitive markets. Value hunters often find more opportunity in lesser-covered races on Days 2 and 3 than in the flagship Grade 1s.

Odds-On Betting in Ireland: Short Prices, Favourites and When They Have Value

Last updated: 4 November 2026

📝 Mark Craic, editor 📅 Updated 4 November 2026⏱ 6 min read

What Does Odds-On Mean?

Odds-on means your potential profit is less than your stake. If a horse is priced at 1/2 (1.5 decimal), you must stake €20 to profit €10. The selection is odds-on because the market implies it has better than 50% probability of winning. Very short prices like 1/4 (1.25) or 2/9 (1.22) represent near-certainties in the market’s view, though sporting events are never truly certain.

The Maths of Backing Short Prices

The key insight with odds-on betting is that your upside is limited relative to your downside. At 1/2 (1.5), a €100 win nets €50. A loss costs €100. You need to win at least twice as often as you lose at this price to break even. At 1/4 (1.25), you need to win four times for every loss. The bookmaker’s margin means that even if you correctly identify a selection with the right probability, the price offered is typically below fair value.

When Odds-On Favourites Lose

Odds-on favourites lose more often than casual bettors expect. The rate varies by sport: in tennis, top-ranked players lose to lower-ranked opponents in Grand Slams with notable regularity; in horse racing, odds-on favourites lose approximately 30–40% of the time. Upsets are built into the mathematics: a selection priced at 1/3 (1.33) has a market-implied probability of 70%–75%, meaning it is expected to lose 25–30% of the time.

Odds-On in Accumulators

Accumulating multiple odds-on selections can produce better overall odds, which is a common strategy for multiples. Combining four selections at 1/2 each produces a fourfold at approximately 5/1 (6.0 decimal), assuming no margin added. However, each loss within the accumulator still voids all legs — and legs priced at 1/2 do lose. Value at Odds-On: When Short Prices Are Worth Taking

An odds-on price is only value if the true probability exceeds the implied probability. Example: a tennis player priced at 1.50 (implied 66.7%) who you believe wins 75% of matches represents value — the market is underpricing them by 8.3 percentage points.

The honest reality: identifying genuine value at short prices is harder than at longer odds, because the books price short-priced favourites most accurately (highest market volume). The exception is in-play betting where momentum shifts create temporary mispricing, and late-market moves that you can spot before prices update.

Lay betting (via exchanges) is the natural alternative: instead of backing the 1.20 favourite to win, lay it at 1.20 — you profit if it loses, risking €0.20 per €1 laid.

What is odds-on in betting?

Odds-on means your potential profit is less than your stake. The selection is priced at below evens, implying the market considers it more likely to win than lose.

Are odds-on favourites always likely to win?

Odds-on favourites win more often than not, by definition. However, they do lose: even a 1/3 shot has an implied 25-30% chance of losing. Never treat odds-on as a certainty.

Should I include odds-on bets in an accumulator?

Including odds-on selections in accumulators is common. Each leg still carries its own independent probability of losing, so the more legs added the more chances for the accumulator to fail.

Why GRAI Needs Substantial Funding

GRAI became operational on 1 July 2026, inheriting responsibility for regulating a gambling market worth over €3.5 billion annually. The authority's core functions require significant resources:

  • Licensing operations — processing applications from hundreds of operators across betting, gaming, lotteries and B2B supply
  • Compliance monitoring — inspecting both online and in-person operators
  • Enforcement — pursuing unlicensed operators and breaches by licensed ones
  • Consumer protection systems — building the national self-exclusion register and Social Impact Fund administration
  • International cooperation — sharing intelligence with EU gambling regulators

How GRAI Will Transition to Industry Funding

The current Exchequer-funded model is explicitly temporary. Under the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, GRAI will move to industry self-funding through:

  • Annual licence fees — based on operator type and size
  • Application fees — for new licence applications
  • Levy contributions — to the Social Impact Fund (separate from GRAI operating costs)
  • Enforcement cost recovery — fines and investigation costs recoverable from non-compliant operators

The transition timeline has not been formally set, but industry sources suggest full self-funding could begin in 2027 or 2028 as the licensing regime matures.

Context: Ireland's Market vs. Regulator Budget

To put the funding gap in perspective: the Irish licensed gambling market generated €911.2 million in Q1 2026 alone. The GRAI's total annual budget of €13.35 million represents approximately 0.4% of one quarter's market turnover. By comparison, the UK Gambling Commission — which regulates a larger but conceptually similar market — operates with a budget of around £25 million per year.
